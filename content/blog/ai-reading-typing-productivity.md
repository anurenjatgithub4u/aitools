# How AI Is Changing the Way We Read and Work: A Practical Guide to Reading & Typing Faster in 2026



A developer I know spends maybe ten minutes writing a function these days. AI drafts most of it. Then he spends the next twenty-five minutes reading through what it wrote, checking whether the logic actually does what he asked for, catching the one edge case it missed, and typing out corrections. He's not slower than he used to be. He's just doing a different job now — less writing, more reading and verifying.

That's the part of the AI story that doesn't get talked about much. AI has made producing information almost instant. You can ask it to summarize a report, draft an email, explain a research paper, write a first pass of code, or put together a presentation outline, and it'll hand you something usable in seconds. What it can't do is read that output for you, decide whether it's right, or communicate the result to someone else. That's still on you.

So there's a bottleneck now, and it's not where it used to be. It used to be generation — the blank page, the slow first draft, the time it took to write something from scratch. Now generation is cheap. The bottleneck has moved to the human side: reading, understanding, verifying, editing, and typing out whatever comes next. A student who asks AI to summarize a 30-page chapter still has to read that summary, decide what's actually useful, and figure out what to do with it. AI didn't remove the reading. It just changed what gets read.

This is a practical look at two skills that quietly became more important because of that shift — reading speed and typing speed — and why they're worth measuring rather than guessing about.

## AI Has Made Information Faster. Humans Still Have to Process It.

There's a difference between generating information and processing it, and AI is only really good at the first half.

AI is genuinely fast at producing things: emails, meeting notes, code, research summaries, documentation, presentation drafts, first-pass ideas. Ask for a draft and you'll have one before you've finished your coffee. That's not in dispute.

But somebody still has to read it. Understand it. Check it against what they actually know. Compare it to other information. Decide what to keep and what to throw out. Edit it into something that's actually correct. And then communicate the result — to a teammate, a client, a professor, a codebase.

Productivity, in other words, is increasingly less about how fast you can produce a first draft and more about how fast and how well you can move from information to understanding to action. That loop — read, think, respond — hasn't gone away. If anything, it runs more often now, because AI generates more raw material for you to run it on.

To be clear, this isn't an argument that everyone needs to read like a speed-reading champion. Comprehension and accuracy matter more than raw speed. A person who reads fast but misunderstands half of what they read isn't actually ahead of anyone. The goal is reading efficiently for the task in front of you, not reading fast for its own sake.

## Why Reading Speed Matters More in the Age of AI

Here's something a little counterintuitive: AI doesn't necessarily reduce how much you read. Often it increases it.

A developer who used to write documentation from memory now gets AI-generated docs to review — more text, generated faster than any single person could write it, and someone still has to read it critically. A student who once struggled to get through a dense chapter can now get an AI summary, but if they're using it seriously, they're also cross-checking it against the original material, which means reading both. A manager who used to write her own reports might now review three AI-drafted versions before picking one. A researcher pulling together five sources with AI assistance still has to read the source material to know if the AI got it right.

So the type and volume of reading shifts. It's less "write everything from scratch" and more "read, verify, and integrate a larger volume of generated content." That's a real cognitive load, even if it doesn't look like the classic image of someone hunched over a textbook.

This is where reading speed, measured in words per minute (WPM), becomes a useful number to know — not as a badge of honor, but as a practical baseline. There's no universal "correct" reading speed, and anyone who tells you there is one number everyone should hit is oversimplifying. A quick scroll through a Slack thread and a close read of a legal contract are not the same task, and they shouldn't be read at the same speed.

Different material calls for different speeds:

- Casual reading (social posts, news skims) can be fast and loose — you're scanning for relevance, not memorizing.
- Emails usually sit somewhere in the middle — fast enough to get through your inbox, careful enough not to miss the actual ask.
- Documentation and technical material often deserves a slower, more deliberate pace, because missing a detail costs you more time later than it would have cost to read carefully the first time.
- Academic and research material is usually the slowest of all — you're not just reading, you're evaluating claims and connecting ideas.

The point isn't to read everything as fast as possible. It's knowing your actual speed at different levels of difficulty, so you can consciously choose the right pace instead of defaulting to whatever habit you picked up in school.

## How to Improve Your Reading Speed Without Sacrificing Comprehension

A few techniques that actually hold up, in no particular order of importance:

1. **Stop rereading every sentence out of habit.** A lot of slow readers aren't slow because their eyes move slowly — they're slow because they reread constantly, often without noticing. Catching yourself doing this is half the fix.
2. **Read in phrases, not individual words.** Skilled readers take in small clusters of words at a glance rather than processing one word at a time. This comes with practice, not effort.
3. **Preview the structure before diving in.** Headings, bolded terms, the first and last paragraph of a section — a 30-second preview tells you what to expect and makes the actual reading faster and more targeted.
4. **Know what you're looking for before you start.** Reading with a question in mind ("what's the deadline here," "what's the main argument") is faster than reading passively and hoping the important part sticks.
5. **Cut the obvious distractions.** Notifications mid-paragraph cost you more than the interruption itself — you often have to re-read to regain context.
6. **Practice with a timer occasionally.** Not because speed is the goal, but because it gives you a sense of your actual pace instead of a vague feeling.
7. **Check comprehension, not just speed.** If you finish faster but can't summarize what you read, you didn't actually save time — you just moved the problem downstream.
8. **Match your speed to the material.** Reading technical documentation at the same pace as a text message is how details get missed. Slowing down on purpose is a skill, not a failure.

None of this is about competing with anyone. Someone reading a codebase's architecture doc should probably be reading slower than someone skimming a newsletter, and that's not a contradiction — that's just good judgment about where your attention is worth spending.

## What Is Reading WPM?

WPM stands for words per minute, and it's the standard way reading speed gets measured.

The math is simple: take the number of words in a passage, divide by the number of minutes it took you to read it. If you read 600 words in 3 minutes, that's 600 ÷ 3 = 200 WPM.

On its own, though, WPM is an incomplete number. Someone can post a huge WPM by skimming and understanding almost nothing, which isn't useful to anyone. A reading speed test worth trusting measures both reading speed and comprehension together — otherwise you're optimizing for a number that doesn't reflect anything real.

## Why Typing Speed Still Matters When AI Can Write for You

This is the objection that comes up almost every time typing speed gets mentioned in 2026: if AI can write the email, the code, the message, why does it matter how fast I type?

Fair question. Here's the more complete answer.

AI reduces how much you type from scratch. It does not remove typing from your day. You're still typing the instructions you give AI in the first place, and vague, hastily-typed prompts tend to produce vague, unhelpful output — the quality of what you type in still shapes what you get back. You're still typing corrections into code AI generated, because it rarely gets everything right on the first pass. You're still taking notes, searching for things, messaging colleagues, filling out forms, and writing the parts of a document that need your actual judgment, not a generic draft.

Put another way: AI may have increased how much work you can generate in a day, but your ability to quickly edit, refine, correct, and communicate that work hasn't become optional. If anything, since there's more raw output moving through your hands, your typing speed and accuracy affect how quickly you can keep up with it.

A slow, hunt-and-peck typist working alongside AI tools isn't obviously behind a fast typist — until you watch them try to fix a bug, refine a prompt, or rewrite an awkward paragraph AI produced. That's when the gap shows up.

## What Is a Good Typing Speed?

Typing speed is also measured in WPM, using the same words-per-minute logic as reading — though the standard convention for typing counts every five characters as one "word," which keeps the measurement consistent even when someone types unusually long or short words.

As a rough, practical guide — not a scientific standard, and it varies depending on the specific test, the language, and how accuracy is weighted:

- **Beginner:** roughly 20–30 WPM
- **Developing:** roughly 30–45 WPM
- **Comfortable:** roughly 45–60 WPM
- **Fast:** 60–80+ WPM

Take these as general reference points, not targets to hit by a deadline. Different typing tests measure slightly differently, and a WPM number without an accuracy number next to it is only telling you half the story.

That accuracy part matters more than people usually assume. Someone typing 80 WPM but constantly hitting backspace to fix mistakes may genuinely be less productive than someone typing a steady, accurate 60 WPM. Corrections cost time too — they just don't show up in the raw speed number.

## How to Increase Typing Speed

A few things that reliably help, roughly in order of impact:

1. **Learn proper touch typing** if you haven't already — keeping fingers on the home row and using the right finger for each key removes a lot of wasted movement.
2. **Stop looking at the keyboard.** It feels slower at first. It's not, once it becomes automatic.
3. **Prioritize accuracy before speed.** Speed that comes from accuracy is durable. Speed that comes from rushing usually falls apart under pressure.
4. **Practice the combinations that actually trip you up**, not generic drills. Everyone has two or three letter pairs or words they consistently fumble.
5. **Short daily sessions beat occasional long ones.** Ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice most days builds muscle memory faster than an hour once a month.
6. **Don't backspace on reflex.** Sometimes it's faster to finish the word and glance back than to interrupt your rhythm for every small slip.
7. **Track your WPM over time**, not just once. A single test tells you where you are. Repeated tests tell you whether you're actually improving.
8. **Practice with real sentences**, not just random character strings — typing real language builds the pattern recognition that actually shows up in day-to-day work.

## Reading Speed vs Typing Speed: Which Matters More?

Neither. They're not competing for the same job.

Reading takes you from information to understanding. Typing takes you from understanding to communication or action. You can think of the whole loop as:

**READ → THINK → WRITE → ACT**

A developer reads AI-generated code and documentation, thinks through whether it's correct, types out fixes or new code, and ships it. A student reads source material and AI-generated notes, thinks about what actually matters for the exam, and writes their own summary or answer. An office worker reads a stack of reports and messages, decides what needs a response, and types that response quickly enough to keep the day moving. A researcher reads across sources, thinks critically about conflicting claims, and writes up findings that need to hold up to scrutiny. A job seeker reads a role description carefully, thinks about how their experience actually fits, and types an application that doesn't read like it was dashed off in two minutes.

In every one of those cases, being slow at either end of the loop — reading or typing — slows down everything downstream. They're not rivals. They're the two ends of the same pipe.

## How AI Can Help You Improve These Skills

Here's a genuinely useful way to think about AI in this context: not as a replacement for reading and typing, but as a coach for getting better at them.

For reading, that could look like:

- Generating practice passages at a difficulty level that actually matches where you are
- Creating comprehension questions to check whether you actually understood what you read, not just skimmed it
- Spotting the topics or formats where your comprehension consistently drops
- Recommending practice material suited to your weak spots
- Adjusting difficulty automatically as you improve

For typing, similarly:

- Flagging the specific words or letter combinations you keep mistyping
- Generating targeted drills around those exact patterns
- Catching recurring errors instead of treating every mistake as random
- Adjusting practice difficulty as your speed and accuracy change
- Building exercises around your actual weak points instead of generic drills everyone gets

The useful loop here is **measure → practice → analyze → adapt**. AI is well-suited to the analyze-and-adapt part — spotting patterns in your performance that you wouldn't notice on your own. It's not well-suited to doing the reading or typing for you, because that defeats the point of practicing the skill in the first place.

## AI Productivity Doesn't Mean Letting AI Do Everything

It's worth saying plainly: good AI productivity isn't "hand everything to AI and stop thinking about it." That's a fast way to produce a lot of low-quality work quickly.

The more useful approach is using AI for the parts where automation genuinely helps — drafting, summarizing, generating boilerplate — while keeping certain things firmly in human hands: judgment, verification, decision-making, real understanding, and how something gets communicated to another person. Quality control doesn't delegate well.

A developer who accepts AI-generated code without reading it is signing up for bugs they didn't write and won't recognize later. A student who memorizes an AI-generated explanation without actually reading and understanding the source material is going to struggle the moment a question is phrased slightly differently than they expected. A professional who sends an AI-drafted email without reading it first is one autocomplete slip away from a genuinely awkward conversation.

The faster AI gets at generating information, the more that human bottleneck — the reading, the evaluating, the deciding — becomes the actual constraint on how much gets done well. Speeding up your side of that loop is not a small thing.

## What These Skills Look Like in 2026

AI is going to keep cutting the time it takes to produce certain kinds of content — that trend isn't reversing. What that means, practically, is that the valuable human skills shift toward the parts AI still can't do for you: processing information quickly and accurately, thinking critically about what you're given, communicating clearly, exercising judgment, staying focused, adapting to new tools, and verifying that what you're looking at is actually correct.

Reading and typing aren't old-fashioned skills that AI is making obsolete. They're the basic interface through which you interact with information and with the AI tools themselves. Every prompt you write is typing. Every piece of AI output you review is reading. Getting faster and more accurate at both doesn't compete with using AI well — it's part of what using AI well actually requires.

## FAQ

**What is a good typing speed in 2026?**
As a rough guide, 40–60 WPM is a comfortable working pace for most people, with 60–80+ WPM considered fast. These are practical benchmarks, not fixed standards — accuracy matters just as much as the raw number.

**What is a good reading speed?**
It depends heavily on the material. There's no single "correct" WPM. What matters more is whether your comprehension holds up at your current pace, and whether you can adjust your speed for harder material.

**How is typing speed calculated?**
Typing speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), using the standard convention that five characters count as one word. It's calculated as characters typed divided by five, divided by minutes elapsed.

**How is reading speed calculated?**
Reading WPM is the number of words in a passage divided by the number of minutes it took to read it. For example, 600 words read in 3 minutes works out to 200 WPM.

**Does typing speed still matter when using AI?**
Yes. AI reduces how much you type from scratch, but you're still typing prompts, corrections, notes, and messages. Faster, more accurate typing means less time lost editing and communicating around AI-generated work.

**Can AI help improve reading speed?**
AI can help by generating practice material at the right difficulty, creating comprehension checks, and identifying where your understanding tends to drop — functioning more like a coach than a replacement for the actual reading.

**How can I increase my typing speed?**
Focus on proper touch typing, prioritize accuracy over raw speed at first, practice the specific letter combinations that trip you up, and keep sessions short and frequent rather than occasional and long.

**How can I improve reading speed without losing comprehension?**
Preview material before reading deeply, read in phrases rather than word by word, cut down on unnecessary rereading, and check your comprehension after each practice session — not just your speed.
